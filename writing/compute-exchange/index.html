A Provider’s Perspective of a Compute Exchange

AI compute is scarce and intrinsically valuable, but a lack of standardization and divisibility keeps it from being traded the way oil and gold are. This white paper models GPU resource allocation from a provider’s point of view: pricing an ask against competing bids, the cost of sampling utilization slowly, energy and compute prices as coupled stochastic processes, and a correlation metric for a tokens market.

2Formulation

The following is the formulation of GPU resource allocation: mins.t.​i=1∑n​xi​Bi​i=1∑n​xi​Rj​≤Kij​(∀j∈{1,⋯,m})​ Here, n is the number of GPU providers, xi​ is the amount of compute used from provider i, Bi​ is the bid for compute provider i, j is the resource (among m total resources), Rj​ is the cost of resource j, and Kij​ is the capacity of resource j from provider i. The following are examples of resources that can be considered:

  • VCPUs

  • Memory

  • GPU capacity

  • Bandwidth

  • Duration of use

3Example: Modeling GPU Price with Gamma Distributions

Consider a Poisson process for the incoming bids for compute P(λ). A Gamma distribution Γ(k,λ) can help us model multiple incoming bids, or competitors, for the compute. Therefore, the profit can be modeled as: E[Profit]=(B−C)⋅P(B<B∗) Here, B is the ask from the provider, B∗ is the lowest ask from any competitor provider, and C is the marginal cost of a GPU. This can be seen as the product of marginal profit of winning the bid weighted by the probability of winning the bid, or being lower than the lowest ask. The goal of the provider is to set B to maximize the expected profit.

4Sampling Rate of Resource Utilization

In this section, we explore the effects of providers having slower and faster sampling rates for resource utilization. Suppose R(t) is the function for resource utilization at time t. Then, the revenue loss suffered by the provider due to discretization is: Loss=continuous∫0T​R(t)dt​​−discretei=1∑n​R(ti​)Δt​​

Suppose that we model this subject to the following Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) with Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM): dRt​=μRt​dt+σRt​dWt​ Solving this for R(t) and E[R(t)], we get: R(t)E[R(t)]​=R0​exp((μ−2σ2​)t+σWt​)=R0​eμt​ Then, the expected loss is: E[Loss]​=E[∫0T​R(t)dt]−E[i=1∑n​R(ti​)Δt]=∫0T​E[R(t)]dt−i=1∑n​E[R(ti​)]Δt​

For the continuous part: ∫0T​E[R(t)]dt=μR0​​(eμT−1) For the discrete part: i=1∑n​E[R(ti​)]Δt​=i=1∑n​R0​eμ(i−1)ΔtΔt=R0​ΔteμΔt−1eμT−1​​

Therefore, E[Loss]=R0​(eμT−1)[μ1​−eμΔt−1Δt​] Taking the first order Taylor expansion, we get: E[Loss]≈21​R0​(eμT−1)Δt The final insight from this analysis is that sampling frequency reduces the loss bias linearly. In other words, if the GPU provider doubles their sampling frequency, they halve the loss.

5Compute Price and Energy Cost Modeling

We can model energy cost using the following jump SDE diffusion model: dEt​=κ(θ−Et​)dt+σenergy​dW1,t​+dJt​ Here, θ is the long term mean price of energy (modeled here for mean reversion), E is the price of energy, dW1,t​ is a Brownian motion, and Jt​ is a Poisson process modeling the energy spikes. We can model the price of compute using the following SDE: dCt​=μCt​dt+σcompute​dW2,t​+ρ(Et​)dZt​ Here, C is the price of compute, dW2,t​ is a different Brownian motion, and ρ(Et​) is some function of energy cost.

If ρ is convex, compute prices are stable when energy is cheap. However, once energy hits a certain threshold, compute prices become hyper volatile. If κ is high, mean reversion is fast and providers can afford to have lower sampling rates for resource utilization. However, if κ is small, the discretization error is wrong for longer.

The profit is given by the difference between compute price and energy cost: Profitt​=Ct​−Et​

Here, we make a small note about the correlation between dW1,t​ and dW2,t​. If high, when energy spikes, the price of compute follows immediately. Therefore, the provider has a natural hedge, since they can pass the energy spike onto the customer faster. However, if low, the compute price might not be as reactive to the spikes in energy cost. This can cause the Value at Risk (VaR) to increase exponentially.

6Correlation Metric for Tokens Market

This section is a small remark about an information-theoretic metric for correlation of tokens in a decentralized compute tokens market. Suppose X1​,X2​,⋯,Xn​ are n different tokens on the market. Due to joint entropy laws from information theory, we know that: H(X1​,⋯,Xn​)≤i=1∑n​H(Xi​) Here, H(X) represents the entropy of X. Rearranging, we get: i=1∑n​H(Xi​)−H(X1​,⋯,Xn​)≥0 This metric, if approximated correctly, can be a proxy for systemic risk in a decentralized exchange. Specifically, this is some measure of correlation between tokens, where a metric of 0 indicates that the tokens are independent.
